\title{Mean-Reversion Portfolio Selection on US-Listed ETFs, 1999--2026:\\
A Replication with Pre-Registered Tests}
\author{Yelnil Gabo}
\date{October 7, 2026\\[2pt] \small Working paper}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\citet{lahanis2025} report that online portfolio selection algorithms built on mean reversion,
CWMR and PAMR among them, multiply wealth by hundreds to over a thousand times on NASDAQ-100 stocks over
1998--2009.
I reproduce their tables exactly from their published code and data, then test the strongest
algorithm, the authors' variant of Confidence-Weighted Mean Reversion (CWMR), on 16 liquid
US-listed equity ETFs with measured spreads and fills that could have been executed. Daily CWMR trades about 350 times its account a
year and trails SPY at measured 2016--2024 spreads. I searched variants that trade less,
selected one on 2016--2024 data and tested it on data it had not seen. A monthly variant
selected this way matched SPY on a pre-registered 2025--2026 holdout (18.28\% against 18.36\% a
year). Variants that learn from weekly or monthly bars passed a pre-registered 1999--2015 test
against SPY: weekly bars returned 9.82\% a year against SPY's 4.90\%. That test turned out to be
weak. An equal-weight portfolio of the same ETFs returned 6.61\%, and the strategy's Sharpe
ratio is not significantly higher than it (block-bootstrap $p = 0.22$). Its lead over equal
weight disappears at about 4.5 basis points per dollar traded, and most of it disappears if each
trade is made one session later. I have no measured spreads for these ETFs before 2016. I find
no statistically significant edge over simply holding the same ETFs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Online portfolio selection treats investing as a repeated game. Each period an algorithm holds
a portfolio, observes the period's price changes and updates its weights by a fixed rule.
\citet{cover1991} showed that one such rule, the universal portfolio, achieves asymptotically
the same growth rate as the best constant-rebalanced portfolio chosen in hindsight. A later family of algorithms bets on reversal instead. Anticor
\citep{borodin2004}, PAMR \citep{li2012pamr}, CWMR \citep{li2011cwmr,li2013cwmr} and RMR
\citep{huang2013} move weight from recent winners to recent losers. The papers that introduced
them report very large wealth multiples on a common set of benchmark datasets;
\citet{lihoi2014} survey the family.

\citet{lahanis2025} implement about 25 of these algorithms and test them on NASDAQ-100 stocks
from 1998 to 2009. Their best result, CWMR, multiplies wealth 1,590 times. They disclose that
they ignore trading costs and that the period may not represent recent markets.

The question here is whether any version of their CWMR survives trading costs and timing on
instruments a retail investor can buy today. The backtests use btest, my backtesting
software, which times orders the same way as my paper-trading system.

\paragraph{Related work.} Trading costs have been studied in this literature from the start.
\citet{blum1999} extend universal portfolios to proportional costs, \citet{li2011cwmr} report
that CWMR withstands small costs on their benchmark datasets, \citet{li2018tco} design an
algorithm that trades reversion against costs, and \citet{li2016olps} publish a toolbox for
these experiments. Closest to this paper, \citet{moon2019} test PAMR, OLMAR and cost-optimised
variants on S\&P~500 stocks over 2000--2017; they find that the standard benchmark datasets
favour mean reversion and that the strategies may fail when explicit or implicit costs exist.
At the level of assets, short-horizon reversal is documented for individual stocks at weekly
\citep{lehmann1990} and monthly \citep{jegadeesh1990} horizons, while industry portfolios show
momentum \citep{moskowitz1999}, so the stock-level evidence does not carry over directly to
sector ETFs. Part of measured short-term reversal is bid-ask bounce \citep{roll1984}, and the
returns to reversal strategies can be read as pay for supplying liquidity \citep{nagel2012}.

This paper adds three things: an exact reproduction of a recent paper that found
implementation defects in it; a test of its best algorithm on instruments a retail investor can
trade, with measured spreads and fills that could have been executed; and confirmatory tests
specified before they were run, with the deviations from that plan reported.
Section~\ref{sec:data}
describes the data and methods. Section~\ref{sec:repro} reproduces the original paper.
Sections~\ref{sec:daily} to~\ref{sec:bars} report the out-of-sample tests in the order I ran
them, since the order matters for how much each result can be trusted.
Section~\ref{sec:deviations} reports where I departed from the pre-registered plans.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses what the evidence supports.

\section{Data and methods}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Algorithm}

All tests use the authors' CWMR as written in their public repository, ported
to btest and checked against their function to within $7.1\times10^{-9}$ per weight on their
data. Their code describes it as a simplified version of the published algorithm
\citep{li2011cwmr,li2013cwmr}. Among other differences, its step size has no confidence term, a
learning-rate factor $\eta$ is added and the update is not mean-centred.
I keep their parameters throughout ($\epsilon = 0.89$, $\theta = 0.92$, $\eta = 0.93$) and
never re-tune them.

Each period the algorithm receives a vector $x$ of price ratios, one per asset ($x_i = 1.02$
means asset $i$ rose 2\%). With current weights $b$ and covariance $\Sigma$ (initially the
identity), it computes
\begin{align}
  \lambda &= \eta \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{b^\top x - \epsilon}{x^\top \Sigma x}\right), \\
  \mu &= b - \lambda\, \Sigma x, \\
  \Sigma^{-1} &\leftarrow \Sigma^{-1} + 2 \lambda \theta\, x x^\top, \\
  b &\leftarrow \Pi_\Delta(\mu),
\end{align}
where $\Pi_\Delta$ projects onto non-negative weights that sum to one. Because $\epsilon = 0.89$,
the update fires unless the portfolio falls more than 11\% in one period, so in practice it
fires every period. Assets that rose most lose the most weight.

I test four schedules (Table~\ref{tab:schedules}).

\subsection{Universe and data}

The universe is 16 ETFs: SPY, QQQ, IWM, DIA, EFA, EEM and ten Select Sector SPDRs (XLK, XLF,
XLV, XLE, XLI, XLY, XLP, XLU, XLB, XLRE). EFA and EEM hold non-US stocks and XLRE holds real
estate. The ETFs launched between 1993 and 2015, so the universe grows over time: it has 11
members in January 1999 and reaches 16 in October 2015. A fund joins on its first trading day.
Before that it holds no weight. Until a fund trades, the algorithm gives it the average price
ratio of the funds that do and splits its weight equally among them, which gives the same
portfolio return, so the algorithm runs on the whole universe from the start and keeps what it
has learned when a fund joins. The universe is a set of large, liquid ETFs chosen in 2026. Funds
that existed in 1999--2015 and later closed or shrank are not in it, which favours any strategy
that holds these funds, including the equal-weight benchmark.

I use two price sources. From 2016 on, btest uses Alpaca SIP minute bars adjusted for splits
and dividends; a strategy decides at 15:30 New York time on data through 15:14 and fills at
15:45, which is how my paper-trading system trades. For runs that start before 2016 I use Yahoo daily
bars adjusted for dividends and splits; a strategy decides on a session's close and fills at
the next session's open. The stored Yahoo closes match a fresh download to within a ratio of
$3\times10^{-6}$ on every date.

\subsection{Costs and benchmarks}

Every dollar traded pays a flat cost in basis points (bp), plus the SEC fee on sales
(\$20.60 per million dollars sold). Turnover is dollars bought plus dollars sold per year,
divided by average equity. I
measured half-spreads at 15:45 from Alpaca quotes on 45 sessions in 2016--2024
(Table~\ref{tab:spreads}).

I charge 0.7~bp unless stated and report results at 5, 10 and 20~bp. The model has no
commissions, no market impact and no taxes; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses each.

The benchmarks are SPY with dividends reinvested and an equal-weight portfolio (EW) of the same
ETFs, reset at each month end. EW differs from the constant rebalanced portfolio (CRP) of
Section~\ref{sec:repro}, which resets every day. EW matters more. A strategy that beats SPY only because
these ETFs beat SPY has learned nothing.

\subsection{Statistics and procedure}

I compare Sharpe ratios with the test of \citet{jobson1981} with the correction of
\citet{memmel2003}, on daily returns of two strategies over the same days. The test uses their
correlation, 0.71 to 0.98 across this study, so it can detect smaller differences than
comparing two independent estimates. It assumes independent, normally distributed returns, and
these daily returns have fat tails and clustered volatility. I therefore also report a
moving-block bootstrap \citep{kunsch1989} with 21-day blocks and 10,000 draws, with a 95\%
interval for the annual Sharpe difference; \citet{ledoit2008} discuss related robust tests.
The tests compare Sharpe ratios of raw returns; the tables report Sharpe ratios in excess of
the 3-month Treasury bill rate.

Each confirmatory test was written into the research log and committed to git before it ran.
The commit names the strategy version, window, settings and pass criterion, and states that the
strategy will not be changed and rerun on that window. Each commit precedes its run; the
holdout's commit, for example, is 28 seconds older than the run's database record. These
records are in my private repository, and git timestamps come from my
machine, so readers cannot verify them independently. I kept 2025-01-01 onward as a holdout.
Two unrelated single-ETF strategies had been run on it before this study; no portfolio
selection strategy had, and I ran one test on it.

\section{Reproducing the original paper}\label{sec:repro}

The authors' repository contains their data, 93 NASDAQ-100 stocks from 1998-01-02 to
2009-12-31. Running their functions reproduces their Tables~3 and~4 exactly: CWMR 1,590.38$\times$,
PAMR 788.26$\times$, Anticor 36.89$\times$, FTRL 15.25$\times$ and the equal-weight constant
rebalanced portfolio (CRP) 12.16$\times$.

The paper describes walk-forward validation of its parameters, but the validation windows lie
inside 1998--2009 and its Tables~3 and~4 report the whole sample, so these are in-sample
results. The paper gives its sample as 1998--2010; the data in the repository ends on
2009-12-31. Within
the sample, mean reversion keeps much of its return under frictions (Table~\ref{tab:paper}).

Four of the paper's strategies differ from the algorithms they are named after. As implemented,
OLMAR and exponential gradient never change their target weights. Both reduce to CRP, and the

published methods. A fifth result depends on the data: FTRL's lead over CRP disappears when the

CWMR's final wealth.

\section{Daily trading, 2016--2024}\label{sec:daily}

On the 16 ETFs, deciding at 15:30 and filling at 15:45, daily CWMR returns 16.6\% a year before costs and
13.8\% at 0.7~bp, against SPY's 14.5\%. It turns over about 350 times its account a year, so
each basis point of cost takes roughly 3.5 percentage points off the annual return. Its break-even
cost against SPY lies between 0.43 and 0.70~bp. Paired tests against SPY are not significant at
any setting I tested ($p = 0.51$ for the paper's close-to-close timing before costs). Daily CWMR
is not worth pursuing at retail costs, and the remaining sections test schedules that trade less.

\section{Selecting a lower-turnover variant, and the holdout}\label{sec:holdout}

I ran the paper's 13 strategy families on 2016--2024 with daily trading, with a daily signal
and monthly trades, and (for 12 of them) with monthly bars: 38 configurations. CWMR with a daily
signal and monthly trades did best,
18.3\% a year (Sharpe 0.87) against SPY's 14.5\% (0.73), with turnover cut to 18$\times$. Its
paired $p$ against SPY was 0.24, and it was the best of 25 lower-turnover configurations, so I
treated it as a hypothesis and pre-registered a test on the 2025--2026 holdout. It matched SPY's
return (Table~\ref{tab:holdout}).

The paired $p$ was 0.77, and the strategy beat SPY in 8 of 21 months. With 438 sessions the test

fresh in January 2016. Run on daily ETF data from 1995 with its state carried forward, the same
strategy returns 11.2\% over 2016--2024 against SPY's 14.4\%.

\section{Learning from weekly and monthly bars, 1999--2024}\label{sec:bars}

The monthly variant above learns from daily moves but holds for a month. Short-horizon reversal
in stock returns is documented over weeks \citep{lehmann1990} and a month \citep{jegadeesh1990},
so a signal built from one day's moves and held for a month measures one horizon and bets on
another. Variants that learn from week-end or month-end prices remove that mismatch. I proposed them
after the holdout result above. On 2016--2024 minute data, with the algorithm started in
January 2016, they returned 17.6\% (weekly bars) and 11.6\% (monthly bars) against SPY's
14.5\%. Weekly bars beat SPY there only with calendar weeks: started on each of the five
possible offsets of 5-session blocks instead, none of the five did (9.4\% to 13.9\%). Neither
variant had been run on data before 2016. The 1999--2015 window had been seen only by the
monthly daily-signal variant of Section~\ref{sec:holdout}.

\subsection{Trading one session late}

If the weekly lead came from a reversal that unfolds over days, delaying each trade by one
session should cost little. Filling at the open two sessions after the signal instead of one
lowers weekly bars over 1999--2015 from 9.82\% to 7.36\% a year (Sharpe 0.35), and the bootstrap
$p$ against equal weight rises from 0.22 to 0.92. Most of the lead comes from the first session
after the signal.

\section{Deviations from the pre-registered plans}\label{sec:deviations}

Two procedures departed from the pre-registered plans. First, the registered design for the
1999--2015 test of Section~\ref{sec:bars} decided and filled at each session's close, so each
order filled at the closing price its decision had used, which no one can trade at. I moved fills to the next session's open and reran both
variants once with nothing else changed. Before the correction weekly bars returned 11.50\% a
year (paired $p = 0.02$ against SPY) and monthly bars 8.47\%. Both passed the criterion before
and after; Table~\ref{tab:prereg} reports the corrected run.

Second, an earlier pre-registered test of the monthly variant on 1995--2015 filled the years
before each ETF launched with similar mutual funds and indexes, and used the same uncorrected
fills. It passed, returning 11.57\% a year against SPY's 9.33\%. I no longer rely on it. With
each ETF's own history only and corrected fills, the same strategy returns 6.79\% over that
window against SPY's 9.33\%. With SPY as its only fund it has nothing to choose between and
stays in cash; its first trade, into DIA, is in January 1998.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The original paper's results are real, in the sense that its code computes them correctly from
its data. They do not survive the move to instruments and costs a retail investor faces today.
Every schedule I tried either trails SPY at measured costs (daily), matches it on a short
holdout (monthly), or beats SPY by a margin the tests cannot separate from simply holding the
same ETFs in equal weights (weekly and monthly bars, 1999--2015).

The weekly-bars variant is the most interesting result. Before 2016 it beat both benchmarks at
every offset. Several explanations fit. Weekly reversal across sector ETFs may have
been real in a market with wider spreads and slower arbitrage, in which case the profit belonged
to whoever supplied liquidity \citep{nagel2012} and was not available to someone paying the
spread. Daily returns
of these ETFs in excess of SPY show negative next-day autocorrelation before 2016 (median across
the ETFs $-0.065$ in 1999--2003, $-0.092$ in 2004--2008 and $-0.047$ in 2009--2015) and none
after (0.000 in 2016--2024). That pattern fits bid-ask bounce in closing prints
\citep{roll1984}, which a backtest captures and a trader pays for, and so does the drop when
trades are made one session late. And the variant came out of a search over
about 100 configurations across this study (an approximate count), which inflates the best
result \citep{harvey2016}. I have not corrected for it; a deflated Sharpe ratio
\citep{bailey2014} would be the next step. I cannot separate these explanations with the data
I have.
Each predicts the effect fades as markets become cheaper to trade, and after 2016 it roughly ties
SPY.

Before calling it an edge I would need:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item measured or estimated spreads for these ETFs before 2016, to replace the flat cost;
  \item a pre-registered test against equal weight, not SPY, on data no variant has touched. The
        2025 holdout has been used once; data from 2027 on would be clean;
  \item results after commissions for a stated account size, and after tax for taxable
        accounts. At 76$\times$ turnover almost every gain is short-term.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

I reproduced a published set of mean-reversion results exactly, then tested the strongest
algorithm on liquid ETFs. The variant selected on 2016--2024 matched SPY on a short holdout.
Weekly and monthly variants beat SPY over 1999--2015 in a pre-registered test, but an
equal-weight portfolio of the same ETFs passes that test too, and neither variant's Sharpe ratio
is significantly higher than equal weight's. The weekly lead over equal weight needs costs below
about 4.5~bp and mostly disappears if trades are made one session later. I find no
statistically significant edge for this CWMR variant over holding the same ETFs.
