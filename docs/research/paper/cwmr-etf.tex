\title{Does Online Mean-Reversion Beat Equal Weight?\\
A Reproduction and Tests of CWMR on US-Listed ETFs}
\author{Yelnil Gabo}
\date{October 8, 2026\\[2pt] \small Working paper}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\citet{lahanis2025} report that the best mean-reversion algorithms for online portfolio selection
multiply wealth hundreds to over a thousand times on NASDAQ-100 stocks over 1998--2009. We reproduce
their tables exactly from their code and data, then test their variant of Confidence-Weighted
Mean Reversion (CWMR) on 16 liquid US-listed ETFs over 1999--2024, deciding on each session's
close and trading at the next session's open. We charge 0.7 basis points per dollar traded, the
average half-spread we measured at 15:45 in 2016--2024; spreads at the open in earlier years
were not measured and were likely wider. Traded daily, CWMR returns 12.1\% a year against 8.4\%
for an equal-weight portfolio of the same ETFs and 8.1\% for SPY, but it trades 170 times its
account a year, its lead over equal weight is gone at 2.2 basis points, and the lead comes
almost entirely from 1999--2008. Learning from weekly prices gives the same return with less
trading (break-even 5.2 basis points), and most of that lead disappears if each trade is made
one session later. Against equal weight, no return or Sharpe ratio difference is significant
after correcting for the nine comparisons we report, though weekly bars' return lead comes close
(Holm-adjusted $p = 0.052$ with 126-day blocks). Against SPY, weekly bars' return lead is
significant after correction with 63- and 126-day blocks, but not with the 21-day blocks of our
main specification. These 1999--2024 results include the years the weekly variant was chosen on
and assume costs at the open that were never measured, so they do not establish a tradable edge. Weekly and monthly
variants passed a pre-registered test against SPY that equal weight also passes, and a monthly
variant chosen on recent data failed its pre-registered test on 2025--2026.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Online portfolio selection treats investing as a repeated game. Each period an algorithm holds
a portfolio, observes the period's price changes and updates its weights by a fixed rule.
\citet{cover1991} showed that one such rule, the universal portfolio, achieves asymptotically
the same growth rate as the best constant-rebalanced portfolio chosen in hindsight. A later family of algorithms bets on reversal instead. Anticor
\citep{borodin2004}, passive aggressive mean reversion (PAMR; \citealp{li2012pamr}),
confidence weighted mean reversion (CWMR; \citealp{li2011cwmr,li2013cwmr}), online moving
average reversion (OLMAR; \citealp{li2015olmar}) and robust median reversion (RMR;
\citealp{huang2013}) move weight from recent winners to recent losers. The papers that introduced
them report very large wealth multiples on a common set of benchmark datasets;
\citet{lihoi2014} survey the family.

\citet{lahanis2025}, a publicly released working paper, implement about 25 of these algorithms
and test them on NASDAQ-100 stocks; their data run from 1998 to the end of 2009, although the
paper gives the sample as 1998--2010. Their best result, CWMR, multiplies wealth 1,590 times. They disclose that
they ignore trading costs and that the period may not represent recent markets.

The question here is whether any version of their CWMR survives trading costs and timing on
instruments a retail investor can buy today. The backtests use btest, the author's backtesting
software. Every 1999--2024 result on the ETFs is also computed from public data by a separate
backtest engine written for the replication; the spread table, the 2016--2024 minute-data
search, the holdout and the superseded 1995--2015 test come from btest alone. The replication
ports the same algorithm code, so the agreement checks the backtests, not a second
implementation of CWMR.

\paragraph{Related work.} Trading costs have been studied in this literature from the start.
\citet{blum1999} extend universal portfolios to proportional costs, \citet{li2011cwmr} report
that CWMR withstands small costs on their benchmark datasets, \citet{li2018tco} design an
algorithm that trades reversion against costs, and \citet{li2016olps} publish a toolbox for
these experiments. Closest to this paper, \citet{moon2019} test PAMR, OLMAR and cost-optimised
variants on S\&P~500 stocks over 2000--2017; they find that the standard benchmark datasets
favour mean reversion and that the strategies may fail when explicit or implicit costs exist.
At the level of assets, short-horizon reversal is documented for individual stocks at weekly
\citep{lehmann1990} and monthly \citep{jegadeesh1990} horizons, while industry portfolios show
momentum \citep{moskowitz1999}, so the stock-level evidence does not carry over directly to
sector and index ETFs. Bid-ask bounce makes measured prices reverse \citep{roll1984}, and part
of measured short-term reversal in stocks is due to it \citep{kaul1990,conrad1997}; the returns
to reversal strategies can also be read as pay for supplying liquidity \citep{nagel2012}. An
equal-weight portfolio is a hard benchmark for optimised portfolios in general
\citep{demiguel2009}.

Beyond \citet{moon2019}, this paper adds an exact reproduction of a recent paper, which
uncovers defects in its code; a test on index and sector ETFs rather than stocks, filling at the
first published price after each decision and with spreads measured from quotes; equal weight in the same funds as
the benchmark; and three tests specified before they were run, reported with their deviations,
including one we no longer rely on.
Section~\ref{sec:data}
describes the data and methods. Section~\ref{sec:repro} reproduces the original paper.
Section~\ref{sec:results} tests CWMR on the ETFs over 1999--2024, Section~\ref{sec:costs}
asks what costs it can bear, Section~\ref{sec:weekly} examines the weekly variant and
Section~\ref{sec:holdout} reports the holdout test.

\section{Data and methods}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Algorithm}

All tests use the authors' CWMR as written in their public repository, ported
to btest and checked against their function to within $7.1\times10^{-9}$ per weight on their
data. Their code describes it as a simplified version of the published algorithm
\citep{li2011cwmr,li2013cwmr}. Among other differences, its step size has no confidence term, a
learning-rate factor $\eta$ is added and the update is not mean-centred.
We keep their parameters throughout ($\epsilon = 0.89$, $\theta = 0.92$, $\eta = 0.93$) and
never re-tune them.

Each period the algorithm receives a vector $x$ of price ratios, one per asset ($x_i = 1.02$
means asset $i$ rose 2\%). With current weights $b$ and covariance $\Sigma$ (initially the
identity), it computes
\begin{align}
  \lambda &= \eta \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{b^\top x - \epsilon}{x^\top \Sigma x}\right), \\
  \mu &= b - \lambda\, \Sigma x, \\
  \Sigma^{-1} &\leftarrow \Sigma^{-1} + 2 \lambda \theta\, x x^\top, \\
  b &\leftarrow \Pi_\Delta(\mu),
\end{align}
where $\Pi_\Delta$ projects onto non-negative weights that sum to one. Because $\epsilon = 0.89$,
the update fires unless the portfolio falls more than 11\% in one period; in the weekly
1999--2024 run it fired on all but 6 of its 1,563 updates since 1995. Assets that rose most lose the most weight.

\subsection{Universe and data}

The universe is 16 ETFs: SPY, QQQ, IWM, DIA, EFA, EEM and ten Select Sector SPDRs (XLK, XLF,
XLV, XLE, XLI, XLY, XLP, XLU, XLB, XLRE). EFA and EEM hold non-US stocks and XLRE holds real
estate. The ETFs launched between 1993 and 2015, so the universe grows over time: it has 11
members in January 1999 and reaches 16 in October 2015. A fund joins on its first day in the
Yahoo data, which for IWM, EFA and EEM is shortly after launch. Before that it holds no
weight. With fewer than two funds trading the algorithm makes no decision and the account stays
in cash. Until a fund trades, the algorithm gives it the average price
ratio of the funds that do and splits its weight equally among them, which gives the same
portfolio return, so the algorithm runs on the whole universe from the start and keeps what it
has learned when a fund joins. Until DIA starts trading in January 1998 every fund carries
SPY's price ratio, so the weights stay equal and learning in effect starts then. The universe is a set of large, liquid ETFs chosen in 2026. Funds
that existed in earlier years and later closed or shrank are not in it, which favours any strategy
that holds these funds, including the equal-weight benchmark.

Prices are Yahoo daily bars adjusted for dividends and splits, from 1995 so the algorithm has
history to learn from before 1999. A strategy decides on a session's close and fills at the next
session's open, the first price after its decision; in practice this would be a market-on-open
order. The open is a single published price. For these ETFs' early years we cannot check it
against an opening auction, and the model fills every order at it, including on disorderly days
such as 24 August 2015, when XLK traded 15\% below its open within the session. Results cover 1999-01-01 to 2024-12-31;
2025 onward is a holdout (Section~\ref{sec:holdout}), which used Alpaca minute bars and trades
at 15:45 New York time, as the author's paper-trading system does.

\subsection{Costs and benchmarks}

Every dollar traded pays a flat cost in basis points (bp), plus the SEC fee on sales (the 2026
rate of \$20.60 per million dollars sold, applied to all years, although the rate varied). Turnover is dollars bought plus dollars sold per year,
divided by average equity. We
measured half-spreads at 15:45 from Alpaca quotes on 45 sessions in 2016--2024, five chosen at
random in each year (Table~\ref{tab:spreads}).

We charge 0.7~bp unless stated and report results at 5, 10 and 20~bp. The model charges no
commissions (Section~\ref{sec:costs} estimates what they would have cost) and no taxes
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), and it ignores market impact, assuming an account small enough
not to move these funds' prices.

The benchmarks are SPY with dividends reinvested and an equal-weight portfolio (EW) of the same
ETFs, reset at each month end. EW differs from the constant rebalanced portfolio (CRP) of
Section~\ref{sec:repro}, which resets every day. EW matters more: it holds the same funds, so a lead over it
comes from the algorithm rather than from the choice of ETFs.

\subsection{Statistics and procedure}

We compare Sharpe ratios with the test of \citet{jobson1981} with the correction of
\citet{memmel2003}, on daily returns of two strategies over the same days. The test uses their
correlation, 0.71 to 0.98 across this study, so it can detect smaller differences than
comparing two independent estimates. It assumes independent, normally distributed returns, and
these daily returns have fat tails and clustered volatility. We therefore also report a
moving-block bootstrap \citep{kunsch1989} with 21-day blocks and 10,000 draws, with a 95\%
interval for the annual Sharpe difference, and show how its $p$-values change across block
lengths of 1, 5, 21, 63 and 126 days. The bootstrap is not studentised; \citet{ledoit2008} propose a studentised version for
this problem. We give Holm-adjusted $p$-values for the nine comparisons of
Table~\ref{tab:tests}; they do not correct for the wider search described below. The tests compare Sharpe ratios of raw returns; the tables report Sharpe
ratios in excess of the 3-month Treasury bill rate.

Three tests were pre-registered: each plan was written into the research log and committed to
git before the test ran, naming the strategy version, window, settings and pass criterion, and
stating that the strategy would not be changed and rerun on that window
(Section~\ref{sec:deviations}). The texts are in the replication repository with their commit
hashes and times. Git commit times come from the author's machine. For the holdout test, GitHub
records the push at 10:06:06 UTC and the backtest database records the run at 10:06:32 UTC. For
the 1995--2015 plan (commit \texttt{d9a912d}), GitHub records the push at 08:12:12 UTC, before
the results commit at 08:12:49 UTC by the author's clock. For the 1999--2015 plan (commit
\texttt{97b0d4a}) there is no third-party record; the order rests on commit times set by the
author's machine, public in the btest repository. The order of the work matters for what is out of
sample. We first ran the original paper's strategy families on 2016--2024 minute data, chose
the monthly schedule with a daily signal, and pre-registered it for the 2025 holdout, where it
failed (Section~\ref{sec:holdout}). Monthly bars had been part of that first search; only then did we try weekly bars on
2016--2024, which beat SPY there with calendar weeks but not at any of five other week-end
offsets.
Next came the 1995--2015 and 1999--2015 plans (Section~\ref{sec:deviations}). Everything in
Sections~\ref{sec:results} to~\ref{sec:weekly} came after and is exploratory, and 2016--2024 is
not out of sample for any schedule. The 2025 onward holdout had been used by two unrelated
single-ETF strategies before this study and by one test in it.

\section{Reproducing the original paper}\label{sec:repro}

The authors' repository contains their data, 93 NASDAQ-100 stocks from 1998-01-02 to
2009-12-31. Running their functions reproduces their Tables~3 and~4 \citep{lahanis2025} exactly: CWMR 1,590.38$\times$,
PAMR 788.26$\times$, Anticor 36.89$\times$, FTRL 15.25$\times$ and the equal-weight constant
rebalanced portfolio (CRP) 12.16$\times$.

The paper describes walk-forward validation of its parameters, but the validation windows lie
inside 1998--2009 and their Tables~3 and~4 report the whole sample, so these are in-sample
results. Within the sample, mean reversion keeps a higher CAGR than CRP under frictions, though
of the friction rows only PAMR with a one-day delay stays significantly ahead
(Table~\ref{tab:paper}).

Four of the paper's strategies differ from the algorithms they are named after. As implemented,
OLMAR and exponential gradient never change their target weights. Both reduce to CRP, and the
paper reports identical numbers for all three. CWMR and RMR are modified versions of the
published methods. A fifth result depends on the data: the lead of follow the regularized leader (FTRL) over CRP disappears when the
32 tickers with incomplete data are removed. Two bad prints, PCAR in February 2000 and XRAY in July 2004, account for about 30\% of
CWMR's final wealth.

Against SPY, daily and weekly bars have bootstrap $p$-values below 0.05 but Jobson--Korkie--Memmel
$p$-values of 0.06 and 0.10. The bootstrap $p$ depends on the block length. The daily return
difference between each variant and equal weight is negatively autocorrelated (lag-1 $-0.04$ to
$-0.09$), and for weekly bars the dependence spans several weeks (variance ratios of 0.68 at 21
days and 0.50 at 126), so short blocks overstate its variance and longer blocks mostly give
smaller $p$-values. With 126-day
blocks, weekly bars reaches 0.048 against equal weight and 0.012 against SPY. After a Holm
correction for the nine comparisons, the smallest $p$ at any block length is 0.11 (weekly bars
against SPY, 126-day blocks), and against equal weight it is 0.35.

The return leads have smaller $p$-values than the Sharpe differences. With the same bootstrap on daily log returns
and 21-day blocks, daily CWMR's lead over equal weight is $+3.4$ percentage points a year (95\%
interval $+0.6$ to $+6.3$, $p = 0.021$), and weekly bars' the same ($+0.6$ to $+6.3$,
$p = 0.021$). Across block lengths the unadjusted $p$ for these two leads runs from 0.006 to
0.051. After a Holm correction for the nine comparisons, the leads over equal weight stay above
0.05 at every block length, though weekly bars reaches 0.052 with 126-day blocks and 0.072 with
63-day blocks. Weekly bars' lead over SPY is significant after correction with 63- and 126-day
blocks (0.043 and 0.024; unadjusted 0.005 and 0.003), but not with the 21-day blocks of our main
specification (0.085). The longer blocks fit the negative autocorrelation of the return
difference, but choosing among block lengths after seeing the results is itself a selection,
which Holm does not correct for. Equal weight itself leads SPY by only 0.3 points a year ($p = 0.68$),
so weekly bars' leads over the two benchmarks are of similar size ($+3.4$ and $+3.6$ points);
only the one over SPY clears the corrected threshold, and only with long blocks.

Turnover in Table~\ref{tab:main} is dollars traded over average equity, which weights the later,
larger years. Measured year by year, the daily schedule traded 230 times its account in an
average year and 358 to 410 times in each year from 1999 to 2005, which is why each basis point
costs it more than 170 turnover suggests.

Where each lead comes from matters for whether these costs were achievable. Daily CWMR's lead
over equal weight comes almost entirely from 1999--2008, when it returned 10.3\% a year against
1.7\%; over 2009--2024 it returned 13.3\% against equal weight's 12.8\% and SPY's 14.5\%. Until
2001 US prices moved in sixteenths of a dollar, so a one-tick spread on a \$50 fund meant a
half-spread of 6.25~bp, almost three times the 2.2~bp the daily lead can bear. Weekly bars' lead comes mostly from 2009--2024
(18.0\% against 12.8\%; 3.2\% against 1.7\% over 1999--2008), which includes the 2016--2024 years
on which it was chosen. Monthly bars can bear 16~bp, but its Sharpe ratio is not significantly
better than equal weight's ($p = 0.40$).

Estimating historical spreads from daily prices does not work at this scale. We applied the
efficient estimator of \citet{ardia2024}, which its authors show improves on the earlier
high--low estimators of \citet{corwin2012} and \citet{abdi2017}, to each ETF's daily open, high,
low and close in each year. On 2016--2024, where half-spreads were measured at 0.17 to 1.68~bp,
its yearly estimates range from $-34$ to $+74$~bp and its medians by ETF from $-11$ to $+17$~bp;
for SPY it gives 11.9~bp against a measured 0.17~bp. Daily price noise swamps spreads this small,
so the estimates for earlier years cannot be used.

Commissions add to this. At the 1999 average commission of the ten largest online brokers,
\$15.75 a trade \citep{sec1999}, the daily schedule's 1,000 to 1,190 trades a year in 1999--2001
would have cost \$15,750 to \$18,700 a year, most of a \$20,000 account, and weekly bars' 238 to
313 trades a fifth to a quarter of it. The backtest also buys fractional shares, which retail
brokers did not offer then.

\section{The weekly variant}\label{sec:weekly}

\subsection{Robustness to the day the week ends}\label{sec:offsets}

with blocks of 5 sessions at each of the five possible starting offsets instead; holidays make

Every version returns more than equal weight's 8.37\%, but the Sharpe difference is significant

The weekly variant is also concentrated. After rebalancing it holds a median of 2 ETFs, and one
ETF carries 90\% or more of the account in 36\% of weeks. 

\subsection{Trading one session late}\label{sec:late}

If the weekly lead came from a reversal that unfolds over days, delaying each trade by one
session should cost little. Filling at the open two sessions after the signal instead of one
lowers weekly bars from 12.10\% to 9.51\% a year (Sharpe 0.44), and the bootstrap $p$ against
equal weight rises from 0.083 to 0.84. Most of the lead comes from the first session after the
signal. Regressing each ETF's return in excess of SPY's on its excess return over the signal
week gives $+0.1$~bp per 1\% move from the signal's close to the next open and $-3.3$~bp from
that open to the open after (standard errors 0.6 and 1.2~bp, clustered by week). The reversal is
between consecutive opens, so the cost of trading at the open, which we have not measured,
decides whether the lead was real.

\section{The holdout}\label{sec:holdout}

\end{table}

The paired $p$ was 0.77, and the strategy beat SPY in 8 of 21 full months. With 438 daily returns the test
cannot rule out a small edge either way. Over 1999--2024 the same variant returns 7.72\% a year,
less than both benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:main}): the lead that selected it does not hold
over the longer period.

\section{Pre-registered tests and deviations}\label{sec:deviations}

Besides the holdout, two tests were pre-registered. The first asked whether the monthly
variant's lead over SPY appeared over 1995--2015, filling the years before each ETF launched
with similar mutual funds and indexes. It passed, returning 11.57\% a year against SPY's 9.33\%,
but it decided and filled at the same close (see below). We no longer rely on it. With each
ETF's own history only it returns 6.80\% against SPY's 9.33\%, mostly because it holds cash
until DIA gives it a second fund; from its first trade in February 1998 to 2015 it returns
7.05\% against SPY's 6.03\%.

The second tested weekly and monthly bars on 1999--2015, with CAGR and Sharpe ratio above SPY's
as the pass criterion. Both passed: weekly bars returned 9.82\% (Sharpe 0.45) and monthly bars
8.90\% (0.40) against SPY's 4.90\% (0.25). Equal weight passes the same criterion (6.59\%, 0.33),
and we already knew this basket had beaten SPY over that window when we wrote the plan, so the
criterion was too easy; equal weight was the right benchmark to name.

The work departed from the plans in two ways. First, the registered designs of the 1995--2015
and 1999--2015 tests decided and filled at each session's close, so each order filled at the
closing price its decision had used, which no one can trade at; the 1995--2015 plan's pipeline
check had judged close-to-close fills equivalent to the 15:45 fills and so missed it. This
look-ahead error was worth 1.7 percentage points a year to weekly bars on 1999--2015 (11.50\%
before the correction). We moved fills to the next session's open and reran the 1999--2015 test
with nothing else changed. Second, a later correction to how next-open fills were booked
required one further run, which left CAGR and the pass result unchanged. The holdout decided at
15:30 and filled at 15:45 on minute data, so the error did not affect it, but its plan stated
that no strategy had used the holdout window, which was wrong (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The original paper's code reproduces its tables exactly, but the tables are in-sample, and some
rest on implementation defects and bad prints (Section~\ref{sec:repro}). On these ETFs, with
fills at the next open, the daily and weekly schedules return about 3.7 percentage points a
year more than equal weight at 0.7~bp (3.4 in mean log return, Section~\ref{sec:results};
weekly bars 2.6 averaged over the five 5-session offsets). Neither lead over equal weight is statistically significant
after correcting for the comparisons, though weekly bars comes close; both need costs at the
open below a few basis points, the daily lead comes
from years when such costs were probably not available, and the monthly schedule chosen for the holdout
trails both benchmarks.

The weekly gain comes from a one-session reversal between consecutive opens
(Section~\ref{sec:late}). Daily close-to-close returns of these ETFs in excess of SPY show
negative next-day autocorrelation (median across the ETFs $-0.036$), which fits bid-ask bounce in
closing prices \citep{roll1984}; the strategy fills at the open, so bounce in closing prices
does not explain its gain. Three explanations remain. Opening prices can bounce too: a published
open may be a single trade at the bid or the ask, and a reversal between opens is then a spread
the trader pays rather than a return. The reversal may be pay for supplying liquidity
\citep{nagel2012}, earned only by a trader whose costs at the open are low enough. And the
variants came out of a search of more than 100 configurations on 2016--2024 alone (38 in the
first search, 25 more with weekly trading or bars, 10 week-end offsets and 42 monthly trading-day
offsets), which inflates the best result \citep{harvey2016}. The smallest unadjusted $p$ in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, taken over five block lengths, is 0.003, for weekly bars' return lead over SPY with 126-day blocks
($|z| \approx 3.0$); against equal weight it is 0.006 ($|z| \approx 2.7$). Among 100 independent
tests of strategies with no edge, the largest $|z|$ would exceed 3.0 about a quarter of the time
and 2.7 about half the time. The configurations are correlated, which lowers those chances, so
this is only a rough guide; a deflated Sharpe ratio \citep{bailey2014} would make it precise.

Before calling either schedule an edge we would need:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item spreads for these ETFs at the open over 1999--2024, measured from historical quote
        data, to replace the flat cost;
  \item a pre-registered test against equal weight, not SPY, on data no variant has touched. The
        2025 holdout has been used by one test of this study and by two earlier strategies; data
        from 2027 on would be clean;
  \item results after commissions for a stated account size, and after tax for taxable
        accounts. At 76$\times$ turnover or more almost every gain is short-term, and wash-sale
        rules would disallow many losses.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

On these ETFs, the authors' CWMR returns more than equal weight at a cost of 0.7~bp, but the lead
is not significant after correcting for the comparisons and depends on costs at the open that
were never measured. We reproduced a publicly released set of mean-reversion results exactly,
then tested the strongest algorithm on liquid ETFs over 1999--2024. Traded daily or on weekly bars it returns
about 3.7 percentage points a year more than an equal-weight portfolio of the same ETFs at
0.7~bp. The daily lead needs costs at the open below about 2~bp and comes almost entirely from
1999--2008, when costs were probably higher: before 2001 the minimum price step alone implied a
half-spread of about 6~bp, and spreads at the open were not measured after that; the weekly lead needs costs below about 5~bp and mostly
disappears if trades are made one session later. Against equal weight, no return or Sharpe
ratio difference is significant after correcting for the comparisons, though weekly bars' return
lead comes close with 126-day blocks (Holm-adjusted $p = 0.052$); against SPY, its return lead
is significant with 63- and 126-day blocks but not with our main 21-day blocks. The variant selected on recent data trails both benchmarks over 1999--2024 and
failed its pre-registered holdout test. These results are in-sample for the weekly variant and
assume costs at the open that were never measured, so they do not establish a tradable edge
for this CWMR variant over holding the same ETFs.
