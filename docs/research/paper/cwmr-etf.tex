\title{Does Online Mean-Reversion Beat Equal Weight?\\
A Reproduction and Pre-Registered Tests on US-Listed ETFs}
\author{Yelnil Gabo}
\date{October 8, 2026\\[2pt] \small Working paper, revised}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\citet{lahanis2025} report that mean-reversion algorithms for online portfolio selection multiply
wealth hundreds to over a thousand times on NASDAQ-100 stocks over 1998--2009. We reproduce
their tables exactly from their code and data, then test their variant of Confidence-Weighted
Mean Reversion (CWMR) on 16 liquid US-listed ETFs, with measured spreads and fills that could
have been executed. Traded daily, it trades about 350 times its account a year, trails SPY at
2016--2024 spreads, and has the same Sharpe ratio as an equal-weight portfolio of the same ETFs. A monthly variant chosen on 2016--2024 data failed its pre-registered
test on 2025--2026. Variants that learn from weekly or monthly prices beat SPY in a
pre-registered 1999--2015 test, but an equal-weight portfolio of the same ETFs passes that test
too, and neither variant's Sharpe ratio is significantly higher than equal weight's
(block-bootstrap $p \ge 0.12$ at every block length tried). The weekly variant's lead over
equal weight needs costs below about 4.5 basis points per dollar traded and mostly disappears if
each trade is made one session later. We find no statistically significant edge over holding
the same ETFs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Online portfolio selection treats investing as a repeated game. Each period an algorithm holds
a portfolio, observes the period's price changes and updates its weights by a fixed rule.
\citet{cover1991} showed that one such rule, the universal portfolio, achieves asymptotically
the same growth rate as the best constant-rebalanced portfolio chosen in hindsight. A later family of algorithms bets on reversal instead. Anticor
\citep{borodin2004}, passive aggressive mean reversion (PAMR; \citealp{li2012pamr}),
confidence weighted mean reversion (CWMR; \citealp{li2011cwmr,li2013cwmr}), online moving
average reversion (OLMAR; \citealp{li2015olmar}) and robust median reversion (RMR;
\citealp{huang2013}) move weight from recent winners to recent losers. The papers that introduced
them report very large wealth multiples on a common set of benchmark datasets;
\citet{lihoi2014} survey the family.

\citet{lahanis2025}, a publicly released working paper, implement about 25 of these algorithms
and test them on NASDAQ-100 stocks; their data run from 1998 to the end of 2009, although the
paper gives the sample as 1998--2010. Their best result, CWMR, multiplies wealth 1,590 times. They disclose that
they ignore trading costs and that the period may not represent recent markets.

The question here is whether any version of their CWMR survives trading costs and timing on
instruments a retail investor can buy today. The backtests use btest, the author's backtesting
software, which times orders the same way as the author's paper-trading system (an account
that trades real-time prices with simulated money).

\paragraph{Related work.} Trading costs have been studied in this literature from the start.
\citet{blum1999} extend universal portfolios to proportional costs, \citet{li2011cwmr} report
that CWMR withstands small costs on their benchmark datasets, \citet{li2018tco} design an
algorithm that trades reversion against costs, and \citet{li2016olps} publish a toolbox for
these experiments. Closest to this paper, \citet{moon2019} test PAMR, OLMAR and cost-optimised
variants on S\&P~500 stocks over 2000--2017; they find that the standard benchmark datasets
favour mean reversion and that the strategies may fail when explicit or implicit costs exist.
At the level of assets, short-horizon reversal is documented for individual stocks at weekly
\citep{lehmann1990} and monthly \citep{jegadeesh1990} horizons, while industry portfolios show
momentum \citep{moskowitz1999}, so the stock-level evidence does not carry over directly to
sector and index ETFs. Bid-ask bounce makes measured prices reverse \citep{roll1984}, and part
of measured short-term reversal in stocks is due to it \citep{kaul1990,conrad1997}; the returns
to reversal strategies can also be read as pay for supplying liquidity \citep{nagel2012}. An
equal-weight portfolio is a hard benchmark for optimised portfolios in general
\citep{demiguel2009}.

Beyond \citet{moon2019}, this paper adds an exact reproduction of a recent paper, which
uncovers defects in its code; a test on index and sector ETFs rather than stocks, with spreads
measured from quotes and fills that could have been executed; equal weight in the same funds as
the benchmark; and confirmatory tests specified before they were run, with the deviations from
that plan reported.
Section~\ref{sec:data}
describes the data and methods. Section~\ref{sec:repro} reproduces the original paper.
Sections~\ref{sec:daily} to~\ref{sec:bars} report the out-of-sample tests in the order we ran
them, since the order matters for how much each result can be trusted.
Section~\ref{sec:deviations} reports where we departed from the pre-registered plans.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses what the evidence supports.

\section{Data and methods}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Algorithm}

All tests use the authors' CWMR as written in their public repository, ported
to btest and checked against their function to within $7.1\times10^{-9}$ per weight on their
data. Their code describes it as a simplified version of the published algorithm
\citep{li2011cwmr,li2013cwmr}. Among other differences, its step size has no confidence term, a
learning-rate factor $\eta$ is added and the update is not mean-centred.
We keep their parameters throughout ($\epsilon = 0.89$, $\theta = 0.92$, $\eta = 0.93$) and
never re-tune them.

Each period the algorithm receives a vector $x$ of price ratios, one per asset ($x_i = 1.02$
means asset $i$ rose 2\%). With current weights $b$ and covariance $\Sigma$ (initially the
identity), it computes
\begin{align}
  \lambda &= \eta \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{b^\top x - \epsilon}{x^\top \Sigma x}\right), \\
  \mu &= b - \lambda\, \Sigma x, \\
  \Sigma^{-1} &\leftarrow \Sigma^{-1} + 2 \lambda \theta\, x x^\top, \\
  b &\leftarrow \Pi_\Delta(\mu),
\end{align}
where $\Pi_\Delta$ projects onto non-negative weights that sum to one. Because $\epsilon = 0.89$,
the update fires unless the portfolio falls more than 11\% in one period; in the weekly
1999--2024 run it fired on all but 0.4\% of updates. Assets that rose most lose the most weight.

We test four schedules (Table~\ref{tab:schedules}).

\subsection{Universe and data}

The universe is 16 ETFs: SPY, QQQ, IWM, DIA, EFA, EEM and ten Select Sector SPDRs (XLK, XLF,
XLV, XLE, XLI, XLY, XLP, XLU, XLB, XLRE). EFA and EEM hold non-US stocks and XLRE holds real
estate. The ETFs launched between 1993 and 2015, so the universe grows over time: it has 11
members in January 1999 and reaches 16 in October 2015. A fund joins on its first day in the
Yahoo data, which for IWM, EFA and EEM is a few sessions after launch. Before that it holds no
weight. With fewer than two funds trading the algorithm makes no decision and the account stays
in cash. Until a fund trades, the algorithm gives it the average price
ratio of the funds that do and splits its weight equally among them, which gives the same
portfolio return, so the algorithm runs on the whole universe from the start and keeps what it
has learned when a fund joins. The universe is a set of large, liquid ETFs chosen in 2026. Funds
that existed in 1999--2015 and later closed or shrank are not in it, which favours any strategy
that holds these funds, including the equal-weight benchmark.

We use two price sources. From 2016 on, btest uses Alpaca's minute bars from the consolidated
tape (SIP, every US exchange's trades) adjusted for splits
and dividends; a strategy decides at 15:30 New York time on data through 15:14 and fills at
15:45, which is how the author's paper-trading system trades. For runs that start before 2016 we use Yahoo daily
bars adjusted for dividends and splits; a strategy decides on a session's close and fills at
the next session's open. The stored Yahoo closes match a fresh download to within a ratio of
$3\times10^{-6}$ on every date.

Every dollar traded pays a flat cost in basis points (bp), plus the SEC fee on sales (the 2026
rate of \$20.60 per million dollars sold, applied to all years). Turnover is dollars bought plus dollars sold per year,
divided by average equity. We
measured half-spreads at 15:45 from Alpaca quotes on 45 sessions in 2016--2024, five chosen at
random in each year (Table~\ref{tab:spreads}).

We charge 0.7~bp unless stated and report results at 5, 10 and 20~bp. The model has no

The benchmarks are SPY with dividends reinvested and an equal-weight portfolio (EW) of the same
ETFs, reset at each month end. EW differs from the constant rebalanced portfolio (CRP) of
Section~\ref{sec:repro}, which resets every day. EW matters more. A strategy that beats SPY only because
these ETFs beat SPY has learned nothing.

\subsection{Statistics and procedure}

We compare Sharpe ratios with the test of \citet{jobson1981} with the correction of
\citet{memmel2003}, on daily returns of two strategies over the same days. The test uses their
correlation, 0.71 to 0.98 across this study, so it can detect smaller differences than
comparing two independent estimates. It assumes independent, normally distributed returns, and
these daily returns have fat tails and clustered volatility. We therefore also report a
moving-block bootstrap \citep{kunsch1989} with 21-day blocks and 10,000 draws, with a 95\%
interval for the annual Sharpe difference, and show how its $p$-values change with the block
length. The bootstrap is not studentised; \citet{ledoit2008} propose a studentised version for
this problem. Where several comparisons are made we also give Holm-adjusted $p$-values.
The tests compare Sharpe ratios of raw returns; the tables report Sharpe ratios in excess of
the 3-month Treasury bill rate.

Each confirmatory test was written into the research log and committed to git before it ran.
The commit names the strategy version, window, settings and pass criterion, and states that the
strategy will not be changed and rerun on that window. The texts of the three plans are in the
replication repository, each with its commit hash and time, copied word for word. Git commit
times come from the author's machine. For the holdout test, GitHub records the push at 10:06:06
UTC and the backtest database records the run at 10:06:32 UTC. For the other two plans the
order rests on the history of the btest repository, which is public (commits \texttt{d9a912d}
and \texttt{97b0d4a}). Everything
else reported for the 1999--2015 window (the comparison with equal weight, the tests in
Table~\ref{tab:tests}, Sections~\ref{sec:offsets} to~\ref{sec:late}) was added after the
pre-registered runs and is exploratory. We kept 2025-01-01 onward as a holdout.
Two unrelated single-ETF strategies had been run on it before this study; no portfolio
selection strategy had, and we ran one test on it.

\section{Reproducing the original paper}\label{sec:repro}

The authors' repository contains their data, 93 NASDAQ-100 stocks from 1998-01-02 to
2009-12-31. Running their functions reproduces Tables~3 and~4 of \citet{lahanis2025} exactly: CWMR 1,590.38$\times$,
PAMR 788.26$\times$, Anticor 36.89$\times$, FTRL 15.25$\times$ and the equal-weight constant
rebalanced portfolio (CRP) 12.16$\times$.

The paper describes walk-forward validation of its parameters, but the validation windows lie
inside 1998--2009 and its Tables~3 and~4 report the whole sample, so these are in-sample
results. Within
the sample, mean reversion keeps much of its return under frictions (Table~\ref{tab:paper}).

Four of the paper's strategies differ from the algorithms they are named after. As implemented,

CWMR's final wealth.

\section{Daily trading, 2016--2024}\label{sec:daily}

On the 16 ETFs, deciding at 15:30 and filling at 15:45, daily CWMR returns 16.0\% a year before costs and
13.2\% at 0.7~bp, against SPY's 14.5\%. It turns over about 350 times its account a year, so
each basis point of cost takes roughly 3.5 percentage points off the annual return. At 0.43~bp it
already trails SPY (14.2\%), so its break-even cost against SPY is below that. Equal weight in
the same ETFs returned 11.9\% a year (Sharpe 0.62); daily CWMR's higher return comes with higher
risk, and its Sharpe ratio is 0.01 lower ($p = 0.92$, block bootstrap $p = 0.92$). Paired tests against SPY are not significant at
any setting we tested ($p = 0.51$ before costs with the original paper's timing, which trades at
the same close it decides on). Daily CWMR
is not worth pursuing at retail costs, and the remaining sections test schedules that trade less.

\section{Selecting a lower-turnover variant, and the holdout}\label{sec:holdout}

We ran the paper's 13 strategy families on 2016--2024 with daily trading, with a daily signal
and monthly trades, and (for 12 of them) with monthly bars: 38 configurations. CWMR with a daily
signal and monthly trades did best,
18.3\% a year (Sharpe 0.87) against SPY's 14.5\% (0.73), with turnover cut to 18$\times$. Its
paired $p$ against SPY was 0.24, and it was the best of 25 lower-turnover configurations, so we
treated it as a hypothesis and pre-registered a test on the 2025--2026 holdout. The pass rule
was CAGR and Sharpe ratio both above SPY's. Both came in slightly below, so it failed
(Table~\ref{tab:holdout}).

The paired $p$ was 0.77, and the strategy beat SPY in 8 of 21 months. With 438 sessions the test

showed why the 2016--2024 result was misleading. The 18.3\% depends on starting the algorithm
fresh in January 2016. Run on daily ETF data from 1995 with its state carried forward, the same
strategy returns 11.2\% over 2016--2024 against SPY's 14.4\% and equal weight's 11.8\%.

\section{Learning from weekly and monthly bars, 1999--2024}\label{sec:bars}

The monthly variant above learns from daily moves but holds for a month. Short-horizon reversal
in stock returns is documented over weeks \citep{lehmann1990} and a month \citep{jegadeesh1990},
so a signal built from one day's moves and held for a month measures one horizon and bets on
another. Variants that learn from week-end or month-end prices remove that mismatch. We proposed them
after the holdout result above. On 2016--2024 minute data, with the algorithm started in
January 2016, they returned 17.6\% (weekly bars) and 11.6\% (monthly bars) against SPY's
14.5\%. Weekly bars beat SPY there only with calendar weeks: started on each of the five
possible offsets of 5-session blocks instead, none of the five did (9.4\% to 14.0\%). Neither
variant had been run on data before 2016. The 1999--2015 window had been seen only by the
monthly daily-signal variant of Section~\ref{sec:holdout} and by equal weight, which had already
been shown to beat SPY there.

spreads this small, so the estimates for earlier years cannot be used. Over 1999--2001 the strategy also made 238 to 313 trades a year.
At the 1999 average commission of the ten largest online brokers, \$15.75 a trade
\citep{sec1999}, that is roughly a fifth to a quarter of a
\$20,000 account each year.

\subsection{Trading one session late}\label{sec:late}

If the weekly lead came from a reversal that unfolds over days, delaying each trade by one
session should cost little. Filling at the open two sessions after the signal instead of one
lowers weekly bars over 1999--2015 from 9.82\% to 7.37\% a year (Sharpe 0.35), and the bootstrap
$p$ against equal weight rises from 0.20 to 0.90. Most of the lead comes from the first session
after the signal. That reversal happens in a part of the day a trader can capture. Regressing
each ETF's return in excess of SPY's on its excess return over the signal week gives $-1.4$~bp
per 1\% move from the signal's close to the next open and $-3.3$~bp from that open to the open
after, over 1999--2015; over 2016--2024 the figures are $+2.9$ and $-3.4$~bp. The cost of
trading at the open, which was not measured before 2016, therefore decides whether the lead was
real.

\section{Deviations from the pre-registered plans}\label{sec:deviations}

Two procedures departed from the pre-registered plans, and the published plan texts carry
notes on two further errors. First, the registered design for the 1999--2015 test of
Section~\ref{sec:bars} decided and filled at each session's close, so each order filled at the
closing price its decision had used, which no one can trade at. This look-ahead error in the
registered design was worth 1.7 percentage points a year to weekly bars. We moved fills to the next session's open and reran both
variants once with nothing else changed. Before the correction weekly bars returned 11.50\% a
year (paired $p = 0.02$ against SPY) and monthly bars 8.47\%. Both passed the criterion before
and after; Table~\ref{tab:prereg} reports the corrected run.

Second, an earlier pre-registered test of the monthly variant on 1995--2015 filled the years
before each ETF launched with similar mutual funds and indexes, and used the same uncorrected
fills. It passed, returning 11.57\% a year against SPY's 9.33\%. We no longer rely on it. With
each ETF's own history only it returns 7.32\% with the same fills and 6.80\% with corrected
fills, against SPY's 9.33\%; it stays in cash until DIA gives it a second fund, and its first
decision is at the end of January 1998. Of the two further errors, the holdout plan states that no strategy
had used the holdout window, which was wrong (Section~\ref{sec:data}), and its pipeline check
judged close-to-close fills equivalent to the 15:45 fills, which missed the look-ahead error
above.

This revision corrects three fill-timing errors in the backtesting code, found in a code review
after the first version of October 7, 2026. With minute bars, an order for an ETF with no trade
between 15:45 and the close filled at that day's last earlier trade (92 early XLRE sessions and
one day with missing data); it now does not fill. With daily data, trades filled at the next
open were valued at the decision day's close, which put each night's gap into the wrong day's
return, and a decision on a run's last session filled at its own close. We also now compute
the Sharpe ratio over the standard deviation of excess returns. Daily CWMR over 2016--2024
lost 0.6 to 0.7 percentage points a year (13.8\% to 13.2\% at 0.7~bp); other returns moved by at
most 0.1 points, and $p$-values and Sharpe ratios by at most 0.03. No conclusion changed, and
the holdout run had no affected sessions.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The original paper's results are real, in the sense that its code computes them correctly from
its data. They do not survive the move to instruments and costs a retail investor faces today.
Every schedule we tried either trails SPY at measured costs (daily), fails a short holdout and
trails SPY when run continuously from 1995 (monthly, daily signal), trails SPY over 2016--2024
(monthly bars), or beats SPY over 1999--2015 by a margin the tests cannot separate from simply
holding the same ETFs in equal weights (weekly and monthly bars).

The weekly-bars variant is the most interesting result. Before 2016 it beat both benchmarks at
every offset, and over 2016--2024 five of its six versions still beat equal weight (Table~\ref{tab:offsets}),
while two of the six beat SPY's 14.4\% return there. Its gain comes from a one-session reversal between
consecutive opens (Section~\ref{sec:late}), which persists after 2016. Daily close-to-close
returns of these ETFs in excess of SPY show negative next-day autocorrelation before 2016
(median across the ETFs $-0.065$ in 1999--2003, $-0.092$ in 2004--2008 and $-0.047$ in
2009--2015) and none after (0.000 in 2016--2024), which fits bid-ask bounce in closing prices
\citep{roll1984}; but the strategy's fills are at the open, so bounce in closing prices does not
explain its gain. Two explanations remain. The reversal may be pay for supplying liquidity
\citep{nagel2012}, earned only by a trader whose costs at the open are below about 4.5~bp; and
the variant came out of a search over about 100 configurations across this study (an
approximate count), which inflates the best result \citep{harvey2016}. We have not corrected
for the search; a deflated Sharpe ratio \citep{bailey2014} would be the next step.

Before calling it an edge we would need:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item spreads for these ETFs at the open before 2016, measured from historical quote data, to
        replace the flat cost;
  \item a pre-registered test against equal weight, not SPY, on data no variant has touched. The
        2025 holdout has been used once; data from 2027 on would be clean;
  \item results after commissions for a stated account size, and after tax for taxable
        accounts. At 76$\times$ turnover almost every gain is short-term.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We reproduced a publicly released set of mean-reversion results exactly, then tested the
strongest algorithm on liquid ETFs. The variant selected on 2016--2024 failed its pre-registered
holdout test.
Weekly and monthly variants beat SPY over 1999--2015 in a pre-registered test, but an
equal-weight portfolio of the same ETFs passes that test too, and neither variant's Sharpe ratio
is significantly higher than equal weight's. The weekly lead over equal weight needs costs below
about 4.5~bp and mostly disappears if trades are made one session later. We find no
statistically significant edge for this CWMR variant over holding the same ETFs.
